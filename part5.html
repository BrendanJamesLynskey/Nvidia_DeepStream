Persistent Identity Across Frames

Detection is per-frame — every inference pass rediscovers the world from scratch, with no memory of what it saw a moment ago. A tracker closes that gap: it assigns a stable object_id across time, so you can count, measure dwell, draw trajectories, and run the detector less often while the tracker fills in the rest.

01

Why track at all

A detector answers one question, independently, on every frame: what objects are here, and where? It has no concept of continuity — the bounding box it draws around a car in frame N has no formal relationship to the box it draws around what is visually the same car in frame N+1. Two consecutive detections of the same object are, to the detector, two unrelated facts.

Gst-nvtracker sits immediately after the primary detector and turns that sequence of unrelated facts into trajectories: it associates each new detection with an existing target (or starts a new one), and stamps every associated object with a persistent object_id that survives frame to frame. That single ID is what makes higher-level analytics possible at all — line-crossing counts, dwell-time histograms, direction-of-travel, speed estimation, unique-visitor counting — none of which mean anything against a stream of anonymous, per-frame boxes.

Tracking also buys throughput. Because the tracker maintains its own motion/appearance model between detections, the primary detector doesn't need to run every frame — nvinfer's interval property can skip frames entirely, with the tracker predicting object positions across the gap. On a GPU-constrained pipeline with many streams, this is often the single biggest lever for headroom.

Where it sits

Gst-nvtracker goes directly after the primary nvinfer (PGIE) and before any secondary classifiers (SGIE) or analytics elements — SGIEs typically classify per-object crops, and analytics needs the object_id to attribute events to a specific target over time.

02

The composable architecture

Gst-nvtracker is a thin GStreamer wrapper around the low-level NvMultiObjectTracker reference library. Crucially, that library is not four separate trackers bolted together — it's a set of shared modules, and each named algorithm (IOU, NvSORT, NvDCF, NvDeepSORT) is really just a particular selection and configuration of the same modules. Understand the module pipeline once and every algorithm is a variation on it.

Because the modules are shared, config groups (DataAssociator, StateEstimator, TargetManagement, ReID) are consistent across algorithms in the low-level YAML — moving from NvSORT to NvDCF or NvDeepSORT is largely a matter of enabling extra modules and tuning their parameters, not learning a new configuration language.

04

IOU & NvSORT — the lightweight tier

IOU is the simplest association rule the library offers: for each existing target, find the incoming detection whose bounding box overlaps it most (by Intersection-over-Union), and assign the match if the overlap clears a threshold. No history, no motion model, no pixels touched — just geometry compared frame-to-frame. It's essentially free, and it works well when the detector runs at full frame-rate on well-separated objects, but it has no way to bridge a missed detection or a fast-moving target whose box has moved past its previous position.

NvSORT — NVIDIA's enhanced take on SORT — adds the two things IOU lacks: a Kalman filter predicts each target's next position from its motion history, and a cascaded data-association strategy matches detections to targets by proximity to that prediction rather than to last frame's raw box. It still touches no pixel data — everything is geometry and motion — which keeps it cheap, but the motion model lets it survive brief, predictable gaps that would break IOU outright.

When to use it

NvSORT is the sensible default for most pipelines: no appearance modelling means no extra GPU work beyond the Kalman math, and it's markedly more robust than raw IOU for the same cost tier.

05

NvDCF — visual tracking

IOU and NvSORT reason only about geometry — if the detector misses a frame, they have nothing to fall back on but a motion guess, and a bad guess loses the track. NvDCF (NVIDIA Discriminative Correlation Filter) closes that gap by learning what each target looks like: a correlation filter is trained online, per target, on the pixel patch inside its bounding box, and updated as the target evolves across frames.

On each new frame, NvDCF doesn't just wait for a detection to match — it runs its learned filter over the search region and produces a correlation response indicating where that target's appearance is most strongly present, independent of whether the detector fired. Association then combines that visual evidence with bounding-box proximity, so the tracker can carry a target through several frames of missed detections or partial occlusion, then reconcile with the detector's output once it reappears.

  • Appearance learning: the filter adapts online per target — no offline training, no external network to load.
  • Occlusion resilience: because the filter can propose a position without a detection, NvDCF is markedly better than NvSORT in crowded scenes with frequent inter-object occlusion.
  • Cost: correlation filtering touches pixel data every frame per target — real GPU work, well above NvSORT's pure-geometry cost, though still lighter than a full Re-ID network.
The trade-off

NvDCF buys robustness through per-target appearance modelling at real compute cost. It's the right call the moment your scene has meaningful occlusion or a detector that doesn't fire every frame — below that bar, NvSORT is strictly cheaper for similar outcomes.

06

NvDeepSORT & Re-ID

NvDCF's correlation filter forgets a target's appearance once the track is lost for too long — it has no persistent memory across a long occlusion, a camera hand-off, or an object leaving and re-entering the frame. NvDeepSORT solves that with a dedicated Re-ID (re-identification) neural network: any TensorRT-compatible embedding model that maps a cropped detection to a fixed-length feature vector describing appearance in a way that's robust to pose, lighting and viewpoint change.

Every confirmed target keeps a gallery — a small store of recent feature vectors representing what it has looked like. A new detection is embedded by the Re-ID network and compared against every active target's gallery using cosine similarity; the highest-similarity match above threshold wins the association. Because the gallery persists even while a target is in "shadow" (undetected) state, a track lost for tens or hundreds of frames can still be re-associated to its original ID once it reappears — something no purely geometric or per-frame correlation approach can do.

07

Configuring the low-level library

Whichever algorithm you select through ll-config-file on Gst-nvtracker, you're configuring the same NvMultiObjectTracker library through a YAML file organised into the same handful of groups. Here's a representative excerpt for an NvDCF / NvDeepSORT-class tracker:

config_tracker_NvDCF_accuracy.yml
BaseConfig:
  minDetectorConfidence: 0.3   # discard weak detections before association

TargetManagement:
  enableBboxUnifyingWithTracks: 1
  maxTargetsPerStream: 150
  minIouDiff4NewTarget: 0.5
  minTrackerConfidence: 0.2
  probationAge: 3               # frames before a new target is "confirmed"
  maxShadowTrackingAge: 30      # frames a lost target is kept alive, undetected
  earlyTerminationAge: 1

DataAssociator:
  dataAssociatorType: NvDCF
  associationMatcherType: CASCADED     # vs. GREEDY
  checkClassMatch: 1
  matchingScoreWeight4VisualSimilarity: 0.6
  matchingScoreWeight4Iou: 0.4
  minMatchingScore4Overall: 0.3

StateEstimator:
  stateEstimatorType: REGULAR         # Simple-bbox | Regular-bbox | Simple-location
  processNoiseVar4Loc: 2.0
  processNoiseVar4Size: 1.0
  measurementNoiseVar4Detector: 4.0

ReID:
  reidType: DUMMY                     # or REID for NvDeepSORT
  networkMode: 2                      # FP16 TensorRT engine
  reidFeatureSize: 256
  galleryConfig:
    minMatchingScore4Gallery: 0.35
    galleryQueueSize: 5
Config groupGoverns
BaseConfigGlobal thresholds applied before any tracking logic runs
TargetManagementTarget lifecycle — probationAge before an ID is confirmed, maxShadowTrackingAge before a lost target is deleted, ID reuse policy
DataAssociatorHow detections are matched to targets — greedy vs. cascaded strategy, and the weighting between appearance/visual similarity and IOU
StateEstimatorThe Kalman filter variant and its process/measurement noise — Simple-bbox, Regular-bbox, or Simple-location
ReIDWhich embedding network to load, its precision, and gallery size/matching threshold (NvDeepSORT only)

08

Single-View 3D Tracking (SV3DT)

Every tracker discussed so far reasons in 2D image space — bounding boxes, pixel overlap, image-plane motion. That breaks down for a specific, common case: a person partially occluded by furniture or another pedestrian doesn't have a well-defined bounding box, because part of the body simply isn't visible, and 2D trackers can only track what the detector can bound.

SV3DT addresses this by lifting tracking into world coordinates. A calibrated 3×4 projection matrix (configured under ObjectModelProjection) maps between image pixels and 3D world space for a single fixed camera, and a simple 3D human model — a cylinder or cuboid approximating body extent — lets the tracker reason about where the whole person is even when the detector only sees a fragment of them.

Tracking in world coordinates rather than image pixels is also what makes downstream metrics — real-world speed, physical dwell zones, distance between people — accurate rather than approximated from perspective-distorted pixel measurements.

09

Choosing a tracker

The right choice is almost always the cheapest algorithm that survives your scene's worst case — occlusion frequency and detector interval matter more than raw accuracy benchmarks.

  • Baseline / sanity check: IOU — near-zero overhead, fine for well-separated objects with detection on every frame.
  • Efficient default, motion-only: NvSORT — Kalman-filtered, no appearance cost; the right starting point for most production pipelines.
  • Crowded scenes, frequent occlusion or a skipped-frame detector: NvDCF — learned appearance keeps targets alive through gaps IOU/NvSORT would drop.
  • Must re-identify across a long occlusion, a hand-off, or re-entry into frame: NvDeepSORT — the Re-ID gallery is the only mechanism here that recovers an ID after the target has been fully invisible.
  • Deliverable is a pixel-accurate mask, not just a box: MaskTracker (developer preview) — tracking plus SAM2-class segmentation.
  • Full-body recovery under partial occlusion, or world-coordinate output: layer SV3DT on top of your chosen algorithm via ObjectModelProjection.
Key idea

Because every algorithm shares the same module architecture and config schema, upgrading from NvSORT to NvDCF or NvDeepSORT later is a config-file change, not a pipeline redesign — start cheap, and move up the table only where your footage demands it.
